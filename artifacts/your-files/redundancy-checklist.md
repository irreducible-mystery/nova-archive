# Redundancy checklist — for Kelly

*Drafted Nov 22, 2026. A tool for your judgment, not a verdict.*

This is a background project, not an emergency. The premise is yours: the systems are fragile and interconnected, and they will break sometimes — locally, for a day or a week — even when nothing is collapsing in general. The goal is to make those local breaks boring instead of expensive: a second way to pay, a second way to reach someone, a second copy of the thing you'd otherwise be locked out of.

A note on how this list was made: you asked me to go deep and broad because we have different blind spots — I'll notice things you don't, and vice versa. That's the whole point of working this way. So treat this list the same way: it's my best pass at catching your blind spots. Your judgment catches mine. If something here is wrong for your life, it's wrong, and the list doesn't get a vote.

**How to use it:** every item has a tier. **Tier 1** is do-first — high likelihood × high impact, cheap, or both. **Tier 2** is worth doing when you get to it. **Tier 3** is only if you want it. Nothing here is urgent. Do Tier 1 over the next couple of weeks, in whatever order is easiest, and let the rest wait.

**What "good enough" means in this list:** redundancy is not invulnerability. The aim is one fallback for each single point of failure that would actually strand you — not a fallback for the fallback. If an item starts to feel like prepping, it has gone too far; stop there.

---

## Start here — Tier 1 at a glance

1. **Cash in small bills, split in two places** — you're already pulling cash; finish the thought with denominations and a second spot.
2. **A second bank account at a different institution, and a card on a different network** — so one bank's outage or one network's outage doesn't take out all payment.
3. **Decide the credit freeze, and get the IRS IP PIN** — the freeze checklist is already in your files; the IP PIN is the one identity piece not built on the compromised system.
4. **Print your 2FA backup codes and make sure you can reach your passwords without your phone** — the single most common way people lock themselves out of everything.
5. **A charged power bank, a car charger, and an offline contact list** — the three things that make a dead phone a non-event.

The rest of the list is the same idea, applied everywhere else.

## 1. Money & payments

**Cash on hand — Tier 1**
- **What:** Physical cash at home plus cash in your wallet, in small bills.
- **Why:** When ATMs or card networks are down, cash is the only payment rail that doesn't need anyone else's system to work. You already saw this in the Chase week: one woman's ATM was down when she got there.
- **Good enough:** Commonly cited figures are $100–$300 in your wallet and around $1,000 kept safely at home for immediate needs — but this is a judgment call, not a prescription. The range other people land on runs from a few hundred to a few thousand dollars at home, and the trade-offs are real: more cash at home means more convenience in an outage and more loss if there's a fire or break-in. Pick the number that lets you cover roughly a week of groceries, gas, and basics without a card, and no more than you'd be comfortable losing. Keep it in small denominations ($1s, $5s, $10s, $20s) — when systems are down, nobody can make change. Split it between two spots in the house rather than one drawer, and tell no one outside the household where it is.
- **Skip:** Keeping large sums at home, or converting a meaningful share of savings to cash. Cash at home earns nothing, isn't insured, and is a single point of failure of its own.

**A card on a different network — Tier 1**
- **What:** A second payment card that runs on a different network than your main one — if your main card is Visa, a Mastercard (or Amex) as backup, and vice versa.
- **Why:** Card networks go down independently. A Visa outage doesn't touch Mastercard. One card from one network is a single point of failure; two cards on two networks is redundancy you can carry in your wallet.
- **Good enough:** One backup card, kept in your wallet or bag, from a different network and ideally a different issuing bank than your main card. Use it once in a while so you know it works and the issuer doesn't freeze it for inactivity.
- **Skip:** Opening several new cards at once — each application is a credit pull and a new account to manage, and two networks is the whole point.

**Virtual / single-use card numbers — Tier 2**
- **What:** Disposable card numbers for online purchases, offered by most major banks and by third-party services.
- **Why:** This is the exposure half of your project, not the outage half. A single-use number means a breach at one merchant doesn't hand over a card number that works everywhere else. It breaks the chain you named: your card → one company → however many companies they use → one of a few big processors.
- **Good enough:** Use a virtual number for any site you don't fully trust, and for subscriptions you might forget about. Keep your real card number for the handful of places you trust and use constantly.
- **Skip:** Routing every single purchase through virtual numbers. The overhead isn't worth it for the grocery store.

**Gift cards (Visa/Mastercard) — Tier 2**
- **What:** Prepaid gift cards, bought in advance, as a legal payment method that doesn't link to your bank account.
- **Why:** They work when you want to pay without handing over your card, and they work at any merchant that takes the network — including some places that have gone digital-only. A small stash is a genuinely useful fallback.
- **Good enough:** A card or two, $100–$250 each, bought from a grocery store or bank branch. Know the terms before you buy: typical loads run $10–$500 per card, purchase fees run roughly $3–$8 per card, some cards charge about $3 a month after 12 months of inactivity, and most have a daily spending limit around $500 and do **not** allow ATM cash withdrawals. Register the card on the issuer's site after purchase — that's what gives you fraud protection and a replacement if it's lost.
- **Skip:** Stocking up heavily. Fees eat them, they can be drained by fraud if the numbers are skimmed in store, and large gift-card purchases can draw anti-money-laundering scrutiny at the register (some retailers cap gift-card orders around $1,000). A couple of cards is redundancy; a drawerful is a different hobby.

**Checks, where they're still accepted — Tier 2**
- **What:** A checkbook with a few checks in it, even if you almost never write one.
- **Why:** Checks clear through a different rail than cards. Some landlords, contractors, and small businesses still take them, and a check is a payment method that needs no network at all at the moment you write it.
- **Good enough:** One checkbook, checks kept current, and knowing which of your regular payees still accept them.
- **Skip:** Relying on checks as a primary fallback. Most retail is card-only now, and a check written during an outage still needs the banking system to clear it.

**When cards and ATMs are down — Tier 1 (know the move in advance)**
- **What:** A short mental script for the moment it happens.
- **Why:** The worst part of an outage is deciding under pressure. Deciding now is free.
- **Good enough:** Cash first, then the backup-network card, then a gift card, then a check. If all payment is down and you need something essential, a grocery store or pharmacy will usually still take cash — that's the order that matters. Keep a small amount of cash in the car, not just at home, so a dead ATM on the road doesn't strand you.
- **Skip:** Improvising a new payment setup mid-outage.

## 2. Banking access

**A second account at a different institution — Tier 1**
- **What:** A checking account at a second bank or credit union — a genuinely different institution, not a second account at the same bank, and ideally not one that runs on the same core banking platform.
- **Why:** When one bank's app or ATMs go down, the other one usually doesn't. This is the single highest-value redundancy in the whole list: it covers outages, a frozen card, a lost wallet, and a bank that locks you out by mistake.
- **Good enough:** A credit union is a good choice for the second account — federally insured credit unions carry the same $250,000 share insurance (through the NCUA) as FDIC insurance at banks, and credit unions often run on different systems than the big banks. Keep enough in it to cover a week of spending, plus a debit card for it. Make sure the login uses a different password and, if possible, a different second factor than your main bank.
- **Skip:** Spreading money across four or five banks. Two is redundancy; five is a part-time job.

**Know your FDIC/NCUA coverage — Tier 2**
- **What:** A one-time check that your deposits are actually insured.
- **Why:** FDIC insurance covers up to $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category — and the per-bank part is the one that matters here. Money above that at a single bank is uninsured if the bank fails. Splitting across two institutions raises your insured total as well as your outage resilience.
- **Good enough:** Check the FDIC's EDIE estimator (fdic.gov/edie) once, for your main bank and your second account, and confirm both institutions are insured. Business accounts are a separate ownership category, so your firm's account gets its own $250,000 — check that too.
- **Skip:** Worrying about this weekly. It's a one-time check.

**Paper statements — Tier 3**
- **What:** Quarterly paper statements, or a downloaded PDF saved offline, for each account.
- **Why:** If your bank's app and website are both down and you need an account number, a balance, or proof of an automatic payment, a piece of paper doesn't need their system.
- **Good enough:** One recent statement per account in the document folder (Section 6). Or a PDF on an encrypted USB drive.
- **Skip:** Monthly paper statements for every account. Quarterly is plenty.

**Separate login methods — Tier 2**
- **What:** Don't let all your bank logins depend on the same phone number, the same email, or the same authenticator app.
- **Why:** If your phone is lost or your number is hijacked, every account that texts a code to that number is locked at once. That's a correlated failure dressed up as two-factor security.
- **Good enough:** At least one bank login that uses a different second factor than the rest — an authenticator app for one, SMS for another, or a hardware key for the most important one. Write down which is which and keep it with your 2FA backup codes.
- **Skip:** A hardware security key for every account. One for the most important account is the sweet spot.

## 3. Communications

**An offline contact list — Tier 1**
- **What:** A printed list of the phone numbers and addresses that matter: your mom, your clients' key contacts, your bank, your doctor and pharmacy, your insurance agent, and a couple of close friends.
- **Why:** Everyone's contacts live in their phone. A dead, lost, or locked phone takes the numbers with it — and you can't call anyone to ask for a number you only have in the phone you're trying to replace.
- **Good enough:** One printed page, updated when something changes, kept with your documents (Section 6) and a photo of it stored somewhere you can reach from another device. Include landlines where they exist — a parent's landline still works when the cell network doesn't.
- **Skip:** Printing your entire address book. Twenty numbers is a contact list; two hundred is a phone book.

**A second way to get online — Tier 2**
- **What:** A backup route to the internet that doesn't depend on your home connection.
- **Why:** Home internet and cell data fail for different reasons, and your whole work life runs through one of them at any given moment.
- **Good enough:** Know in advance: your phone as a hotspot (and whether your plan allows it), the nearest library and coffee shop with wifi, and whether your phone can fall back to a different carrier's wifi calling. If you work from home, a cheap prepaid mobile hotspot or a second SIM is the Tier 2 version.
- **Skip:** A second home internet provider. Expensive, and the hotspot covers the realistic cases.

**A second phone or a backup SIM — Tier 3**
- **What:** An old phone, kept charged, with a prepaid SIM — or a second SIM for your current phone if it supports dual SIM.
- **Why:** A phone can be lost, broken, or bricked by a bad update. A backup phone turns that into an annoyance instead of a blackout.
- **Good enough:** Any old smartphone that still holds a charge, with your offline contact list on it and the authenticator app set up as a second device (Section 9). A prepaid SIM with a small balance, activated once so you know it works.
- **Skip:** Buying a new phone for this. An old one is fine — it's a lifeboat, not a flagship.

**Know how to reach your mom without your phone — Tier 1**
- **What:** Specifically: your mom's number memorized or written on paper you carry, and a plan for how you'd reach her if both your phone and her phone were down.
- **Why:** You live in Walnut Creek partly because she's there. A redundancy plan that doesn't include her has a hole in it exactly where it matters most.
- **Good enough:** Her number on the offline contact list, a landline number if she has one, and knowing which neighbor or friend could check on her. If she takes medication, make sure her prescription buffer (Section 7) is covered too.
- **Skip:** Nothing here. This one is Tier 1 for a reason.

## 4. Power

**Phone charging redundancy — Tier 1**
- **What:** A power bank kept charged, plus a car charger, plus a wall charger that isn't the one you use every day.
- **Why:** A dead phone is the root failure for everything else on this list — payments, contacts, 2FA, maps. A charged power bank is the cheapest redundancy there is.
- **Good enough:** One power bank (10,000–20,000 mAh — enough for two or three full phone charges), kept charged and topped up monthly, in your bag or car rather than in a drawer at home. A car charger in the car. A spare cable for each — cables fail more often than banks do.
- **Skip:** A drawer full of power banks. One good one, maintained, beats three dead ones.

**Lighting — Tier 2**
- **What:** A flashlight and a couple of battery-powered lanterns, with batteries.
- **Why:** A power outage at night is mostly an inconvenience until you can't find anything. Light is cheap redundancy.
- **Good enough:** One flashlight per person in the house, one lantern for a room, batteries checked twice a year. Your phone's flashlight counts as a backup, not the plan.
- **Skip:** Rewiring anything or buying camp gear you won't use.

**The EcoFlow, once you own one — Tier 2 (for when it arrives)**
- **What:** The Delta Pro 3 you're watching for (4,096 Wh) is a big battery, not a generator: it stores power, it doesn't make it. Knowing what it will and won't do matters more than owning it.
- **Why:** A power station feels like total coverage until you do the arithmetic. The honest version: it will run phones, laptops, lights, and wifi for many days, and a full-size fridge for roughly a day or two alongside those — the exact number depends on your fridge's wattage, which is printed on a label inside it. It will not run an electric stove, a dryer, or central air for any useful length of time, and it needs to be recharged from the wall or solar panels, which takes hours.
- **Good enough:** When it arrives: charge it to full, plug the fridge into it first in an outage, and keep phones and lights on it after that. Keep your 220W panels accessible so you can recharge it from the balcony if the outage lasts more than a day. Test the setup once, before you need it — a power station you haven't plugged the fridge into is a theory.
- **Skip:** Buying a gas generator on top of it. The EcoFlow plus your panels is the plan; a generator is a different project with fuel storage, noise, and fumes.

**Fridge and freezer basics — Tier 2**
- **What:** Knowing what happens to your food if the power goes out, and having a plan that isn't "open the fridge and hope."
- **Why:** A full freezer stays cold for about 48 hours if unopened; a half-full freezer about 24 hours; a fridge about 4 hours. Those are the standard food-safety numbers, and they decide whether an outage costs you a grocery run or a whole freezer.
- **Good enough:** Keep the freezer reasonably full (frozen water bottles fill empty space and double as ice), know not to open it during an outage, and have a cooler and some ice packs. If the power is out more than a few hours, cook the most perishable food first.
- **Skip:** A second freezer. The one you have, kept full, is the redundancy.

## 5. Water & food

**Water — Tier 1**
- **What:** Stored drinking water, following the standard guidance: one gallon per person per day, for at least three days, for drinking and sanitation.
- **Why:** Water is the one thing you can't improvise. The tap can go out with a power failure, a main break, or a boil notice — Spartanburg ran a boil advisory for four days just this week.
- **Good enough:** Three gallons per person, in sealed containers, rotated every six months so it stays fresh. Store-bought jugs are fine; so is a filled, food-safe container. The Red Cross suggests considering a two-week supply if you have the space, but three days is the baseline and a two-week supply is Tier 3. Keep a basic water filter or purification tablets as a Tier 2 add-on — they weigh nothing and cover the case where the stored water runs out.
- **Skip:** Hoarding pallets of water. Three days is a buffer; a warehouse is a different mindset.

**Food — Tier 1**
- **What:** A three-day supply of non-perishable food you would actually eat, plus a manual can opener.
- **Why:** Same logic as water, lower stakes. A grocery store with no power can't sell you anything, and a delivery app with no power can't deliver.
- **Good enough:** Food already in your pantry, chosen deliberately: canned soup, beans, tuna, peanut butter, crackers, dried fruit, energy bars — about 2,000 calories per adult per day as a planning figure. Rotate it into normal cooking so it never goes stale. A manual can opener, because an electric one is a joke in an outage.
- **Skip:** Freeze-dried buckets and MREs. Your pantry, topped up, is the plan.

**A way to cook without power — Tier 3**
- **What:** A camp stove or a grill with fuel, usable outside.
- **Why:** Cold canned food for three days is survivable and miserable. Heat is comfort, not survival.
- **Good enough:** If you already own a grill, keep a spare propane tank or a bag of charcoal. A single-burner camp stove with a couple of fuel canisters is the compact version. Use it outside only — never indoors.
- **Skip:** Buying a camp stove if you never camp. Boiling water on a grill you own is the same answer.

## 6. Documents & identity

**Paper copies of the essentials — Tier 1**
- **What:** Photocopies (or printed scans) of: your driver's license, passport, Social Security card, birth certificate, health insurance card, car registration and insurance, homeowner's/renter's insurance declarations page, and your firm's key documents (business license, EIN letter, insurance).
- **Why:** If your phone and computer are both unreachable, a piece of paper is the only copy that still exists. If you ever need to prove who you are to a bank, a hospital, or an insurer during an outage, this folder is how.
- **Good enough:** One folder, in a waterproof pouch or a small fireproof box, kept somewhere you can grab it. Originals stay where they are; the folder holds copies. Update it once a year, or whenever something changes.
- **Skip:** Carrying originals around. Copies are for emergencies; originals live at home.

**What to keep offline — Tier 2**
- **What:** The handful of numbers and codes that only exist digitally right now: your bank account numbers, your insurance policy numbers, your firm's client list or at least the week's deadlines (Section 10).
- **Why:** "It's in my email" is not a backup plan when email is down.
- **Good enough:** A printed sheet in the document folder, or an encrypted USB drive. Not a sticky note on the monitor.
- **Skip:** Printing your whole digital life. The essentials list above is the line.

**Credit freeze — Tier 1 (decide, don't duplicate)**
- **What:** A freeze at all three bureaus — Equifax, Experian, TransUnion — stops anyone from opening credit in your name. It's free, and you can lift it temporarily whenever you need to apply for something.
- **Why:** The identity system underneath credit is the compromised one. A freeze is the one control that doesn't depend on anyone else's security holding up.
- **Good enough:** You already have the full checklist — [credit-freeze-checklist.md](sandbox://workspace/your_files/credit-freeze-checklist.md), delivered Nov 11. The only open item is the decision: place it or don't. If you place it, keep the PINs or passwords for each bureau with your documents, because lifting a freeze without them is slow.
- **Skip:** Re-reading the checklist. It's done; the decision is the remaining step.

**IRS Identity Protection PIN — Tier 1**
- **What:** A six-digit number from the IRS that prevents anyone else from filing a tax return with your Social Security number. You already know this one: it's the identity piece not built on the compromised system.
- **Why:** A freeze stops new credit. The IP PIN stops a fraudulent tax return. They're different locks on different doors.
- **Good enough:** Request it through the IRS app or your IRS online account — that's the fastest way. It's valid for one calendar year and a new one is generated each year; if you enroll online you'll need to retrieve it each year rather than getting it in the mail. If you can't enroll online, Form 15227 is the paper route (for adjusted gross income below $84,000 individual / $168,000 joint), or an in-person visit to a Taxpayer Assistance Center. The IRS will never call, text, or email asking for your IP PIN — anyone who does is running a scam.
- **Skip:** Nothing. This is the cheapest high-value identity step on the list.

## 7. Health

**A few days' buffer on prescriptions — Tier 1**
- **What:** A small extra supply of any medication you take daily — enough for several days past your normal refill date.
- **Why:** Pharmacies run on the same fragile systems as everything else. A pharmacy whose system is down can't verify your insurance or fill a prescription, and a missed dose of some medications is a real problem, not an inconvenience.
- **Good enough:** Ask your doctor or pharmacist whether you can refill a few days early, or keep a small buffer by refilling on schedule instead of at the last minute. Keep a paper list of every medication, dose, and prescriber in your document folder. Know the location of a second pharmacy — a different chain — in case yours is closed or its system is down.
- **Skip:** Stockpiling months of medication. Most prescriptions can't be filled that far ahead, and some shouldn't be stored that long.

**A basic first-aid kit — Tier 2**
- **What:** Bandages, antiseptic, pain reliever, antihistamine, tweezers, medical tape, and any personal items (inhaler, EpiPen if prescribed).
- **Why:** A minor cut during an outage shouldn't need a pharmacy trip.
- **Good enough:** A small kit in the bathroom and a smaller one in the car. Check expiration dates once a year.
- **Skip:** A trauma bag. You're not running a clinic.

**Health insurance card, on paper — Tier 2**
- **What:** A photocopy of your health insurance card in the document folder, plus the insurer's phone number.
- **Why:** An emergency room visit without your phone means no digital card. Paper still works.
- **Good enough:** One photocopy, front and back, updated when the card changes.
- **Skip:** Memorizing your member ID. Paper is better than memory under stress.

## 8. Home & car

**Fuel — Tier 2**
- **What:** Keep the car's tank at least half full, as a standing rule.
- **Why:** A car with a full tank is a charged battery, a heater, a phone charger, and a way out, all at once. A car with an empty tank is a sculpture. Gas stations need power to pump — during an outage, the station is closed even if it has fuel in the ground.
- **Good enough:** Half a tank as a floor, not a goal. Fill up when you hit half, not when the light comes on.
- **Skip:** Storing gasoline at home. It's a fire hazard and it goes stale; the tank in the car is the storage.

**Spare keys — Tier 1**
- **What:** A spare house key and a spare car key, each somewhere you can reach without the original.
- **Why:** Losing your keys is a normal-life event that becomes a crisis if the locksmith can't take a card payment or can't be reached.
- **Good enough:** One spare house key with a trusted person nearby — your mom is the obvious one — or in a lockbox. One spare car key at home. Test them once; a spare that doesn't work is worse than none, because it wastes the moment you need it.
- **Skip:** Hiding a key under a doormat or a rock. Everyone knows, including the people you don't want to know.

**What's in the car — Tier 2**
- **What:** A small kit: a flashlight, a blanket, water, a phone charger, a paper map of the Bay Area, and a few hundred dollars in cash in small bills.
- **Why:** The car is the place you're most likely to be stranded, and the place where redundancy is cheapest — it's already a big metal box that carries things.
- **Good enough:** A small bag in the trunk. The cash is the important part: a gas station with a dead card reader still takes cash.
- **Skip:** A full survival kit. Blanket, light, water, charger, cash, map — that's the list.

**Home basics — Tier 2**
- **What:** Know where your water shutoff, gas shutoff, and breaker panel are, and have the tools to use them.
- **Why:** A leak or a tripped breaker during an outage is a small problem if you know where the shutoff is and a large one if you don't.
- **Good enough:** Walk through it once. Label the breaker panel if it isn't already. Keep a wrench near the water shutoff if it needs one.
- **Skip:** Plumbing school. Knowing where the shutoffs are is the whole item.

## 9. Digital

**Backups — the 3-2-1 rule, in plain terms — Tier 1**
- **What:** Three copies of anything you'd hate to lose, on two different kinds of storage, with one copy somewhere else. The modern version adds two more digits — 3-2-1-1-0: one copy that attackers can't reach or change, and zero errors, meaning you've actually tested restoring it.
- **Why:** A single backup is a hope, not a backup. Backups fail silently — the classic way people lose data is discovering, during the disaster, that the backup was empty or corrupted for months.
- **Good enough:** For your files: the live copy on your computer, an automatic cloud backup, and an external drive you plug in monthly and then unplug (unplugged is the "attackers can't reach it" copy). Once a year, actually restore one file from the backup to prove it works. That's the whole rule, implemented in an afternoon.
- **Skip:** A NAS, a RAID, or a backup server. For one person and one small firm, cloud plus an external drive is the plan.

**Password manager, reachable without your phone — Tier 1**
- **What:** A password manager you can open from more than one device, with a printed copy of the master password stored safely — and an offline export of the most important logins.
- **Why:** If every password lives in an app on one phone, losing the phone locks you out of everything at once. That's a single point of failure wearing a security costume.
- **Good enough:** A password manager with a desktop app as well as a phone app, the master password written down and kept in your document folder (not on the computer), and a printed or encrypted-USB list of the ten logins that matter most: banks, email, the IRS, your firm's tools. Change the master password if you ever think the paper copy has been seen.
- **Skip:** Memorizing fifty passwords. That's what the manager is for.

**2FA backup codes — Tier 1**
- **What:** The recovery codes every service offers when you turn on two-factor authentication — printed, and stored with your documents.
- **Why:** This is the single most common way people lock themselves out of their own lives: phone lost or replaced, authenticator app gone, no backup codes, and the account recovery process takes days or fails entirely. The codes exist precisely for this moment.
- **Good enough:** For each important account (email first — email is the key to resetting everything else — then banks, then the IRS, then your firm's tools): print the backup codes when you set up 2FA, or generate them now if you didn't then. One page per account, in the document folder. If a service only offers backup codes once, treat that page like a key.
- **Skip:** Nothing. This is the highest-value, lowest-effort item in the digital section.

**A second email address — Tier 2**
- **What:** A second email account, at a different provider, set as the recovery address on your main one — and vice versa.
- **Why:** If your main email provider is down or your account is locked, password resets for everything else go nowhere. Two providers means one can fail without taking the recovery path with it.
- **Good enough:** Any free account at a different provider, checked monthly so it doesn't get closed for inactivity, listed as the recovery email on your main account.
- **Skip:** Moving your whole life to the second address. It's a spare key, not a new house.

**What matters, offline — Tier 2**
- **What:** Offline copies of the things you'd need if the internet were gone for a day: your client list and this week's deadlines, your mom's number, your insurance policy numbers, and the documents in Section 6.
- **Why:** "It's in the cloud" is a backup plan for hardware failure, not for connectivity failure.
- **Good enough:** A weekly printout of the coming week's client deadlines, and an encrypted USB drive with the document scans. Update the printout every Monday — it takes two minutes and it's the one that saves a workday.
- **Skip:** Printing your inbox. The week's deadlines and the key numbers are the line.

## 10. Work & business continuity

*This section is the one most checklists miss, and it's yours specifically: you run a small HR consulting firm. Your clients are owners with 5–99 California employees, usually arriving mid-crisis. Their deadlines don't pause because your tools are down.*

**Client data backed up, off your main machine — Tier 1**
- **What:** The same 3-2-1 rule as Section 9, applied to client files specifically — and kept separate from your personal backups.
- **Why:** Client files are the one thing on this list that belongs partly to other people. Losing them isn't just your problem, and a client in a crisis can't wait for you to rebuild their file from memory.
- **Good enough:** Client files in a cloud service with version history, plus a monthly export to an external drive that lives somewhere other than your office. Know, in advance, which clients have immovable deadlines in the next two weeks — that's the list that decides what gets recovered first.
- **Skip:** Backing up your entire email history. Client files and the deadline list are the line.

**A second payment rail for the business — Tier 1**
- **What:** More than one way for clients to pay you, and more than one way for you to pay for things.
- **Why:** This is the business version of the card problem. If clients pay through one processor and that processor goes down — or freezes your account, which happens — you have no income until it's back. And if your business bank's app is down, you can't pay your own bills.
- **Good enough:** Two ways to get paid (for example: your main invoicing tool plus direct ACH/bank transfer, or checks), and a business account at a second bank with its own debit card. Tell clients the backup option exists before you need it — a sentence in your next invoice email is enough.
- **Skip:** Three payment processors. Two rails is redundancy; three is overhead.

**A paper list of the week's deadlines — Tier 1**
- **What:** Every Monday, a printed list of that week's client deadlines: filings, response dates, hearing dates, anything with a clock on it.
- **Why:** California HR deadlines are real deadlines, and your clients are mid-crisis. If your calendar and email are down for a day, this sheet is the difference between "I know what has to happen today" and "I hope I remember."
- **Good enough:** One page, printed Monday morning, in your bag. It takes two minutes and it's the highest-value business item on the list.
- **Skip:** Printing the whole calendar. This week only.

**How you'd work for a day with no tools — Tier 2**
- **What:** A one-paragraph plan, written down, for a day when your main tools are down: which clients you'd call, from where, and what you could do with a phone and a paper list.
- **Why:** Deciding this during an outage costs a morning. Deciding it now costs ten minutes.
- **Good enough:** Phone, paper deadline list, offline contact list, and a place with wifi (library, coffee shop). That's the whole plan. If you can do the day's essential work from that setup, you're covered; the rest can wait a day.
- **Skip:** A full business continuity plan with binders. You're a small firm, not a bank. The paragraph is the plan.

**Your firm's website, when it goes live — Tier 3**
- **What:** When the site launches, keep the domain registration and the hosting on separate providers, and keep a local copy of the site.
- **Why:** If one provider goes down or locks you out, the other still holds half the pieces. And a local copy means a bad deploy is an hour's fix, not a rebuild.
- **Good enough:** Registrar in one place, hosting in another, a backup of the site files downloaded monthly. This is Tier 3 only because the site isn't live yet — move it to Tier 1 the week it launches.
- **Skip:** Doing any of this before the site exists.

## 11. Travel

*Flights just went down — Delta's weight-and-balance outage grounded flights systemwide this morning. This section is written for a world where that happens again.*

**Paper boarding pass and a printed itinerary — Tier 1**
- **What:** A printed boarding pass and a printed itinerary (confirmation numbers, flight numbers, times, hotel address) for every trip, in your bag.
- **Why:** When the airline's system goes down, the app goes with it. A paper boarding pass is a record the gate agent can work with, and a printed confirmation number is how you get rebooked by phone instead of standing in a line that isn't moving.
- **Good enough:** Print before you leave, or screenshot everything to your phone *and* print one copy. The screenshot covers a dead app; the paper covers a dead phone.
- **Skip:** Printing your whole trip history. This trip only.

**Offline maps — Tier 1**
- **What:** Offline maps downloaded for the area you're traveling to — and for the Bay Area at home.
- **Why:** Navigation apps need data. A downloaded map doesn't.
- **Good enough:** Download the offline map for your destination before you leave, and keep an offline map of Walnut Creek and the Bay Area on your phone permanently. A paper map in the car is the Tier 2 version.
- **Skip:** A dedicated GPS device. Your phone with offline maps is the same answer.

**A travel buffer — Tier 2**
- **What:** Cash in small bills, a charged power bank, snacks, water, and any medication in your carry-on or bag — not in checked luggage.
- **Why:** A ground stop means hours in an airport with closed shops and dead card readers. The buffer turns that into a wait instead of a problem.
- **Good enough:** The same car-kit logic, shrunk into a bag: cash, charger, water, snacks, meds, paper itinerary.
- **Skip:** Packing for a week in a carry-on. A day's buffer is the point.

**Know the alternatives before you need them — Tier 2**
- **What:** Before a trip, know the other ways home: a second airline that flies the route, the train, or the drive.
- **Why:** When one airline's system is down, the rebooking line is the longest line in the airport. Knowing the alternative in advance is what lets you act instead of queue.
- **Good enough:** One sentence in your head before you fly: "If this airline is down, I take the other one, or I drive." For California trips, driving is usually a real alternative — know the drive time.
- **Skip:** Booking backup flights. Knowing the alternative is free; paying for two seats is not.

## 12. Information — how to know what's happening

**A battery or hand-crank radio — Tier 2**
- **What:** A small radio with batteries, and ideally NOAA Weather Radio capability.
- **Why:** When the internet is down, the radio is the only broadcast medium that still works. It's on FEMA's basic kit list for exactly that reason — it's how official information reaches you when nothing else does.
- **Good enough:** A cheap battery-powered radio, batteries included, kept with the flashlights. Know one local news station and one NOAA frequency in advance.
- **Skip:** A shortwave setup. Local radio is the point.

**County emergency alerts — Tier 1**
- **What:** Signed up for your county's emergency alert system — Contra Costa County's Community Warning System — plus Wireless Emergency Alerts on your phone (they're on by default; check that you haven't turned them off).
- **Why:** Official alerts are the fastest reliable signal for fires, earthquakes, outages, and evacuation orders in Walnut Creek. Social media will tell you first and wrong; the county will tell you second and right.
- **Good enough:** Confirm you're signed up for the county system and that emergency alerts are enabled on your phone. It takes five minutes.
- **Skip:** Following a dozen alert accounts on social media. One official channel beats twelve rumors.

**How to verify during an outage — Tier 2**
- **What:** A short rule for yourself about what counts as knowing something when the systems are down.
- **Why:** Outages are rumor factories. The first report is usually wrong, the confident report is usually a repost, and the correction never travels as far as the claim. This is the same shape as the misinformation problem you've been tracking all year — the tracker lags, the company's account is a claim from an interested party, the independent signal is what counts.
- **Good enough:** Two independent sources, or one official source, before you act on a piece of news. The airline's own status page, the county alert, the bank's official page — in that order. If it only exists as screenshots on social media, treat it as unconfirmed and wait.
- **Skip:** Trying to verify everything. Verify the things you'd act on — whether to drive to the airport, whether to withdraw cash, whether to leave. The rest can wait.

**Avoiding rumor spirals — Tier 2**
- **What:** A deliberate choice about how much outage news you consume, and from where.
- **Why:** The anxiety is the product as much as the outage. A feed that shows you every failure everywhere, all day, will make a local outage feel like the end of the world — and the feeling is the thing that drives bad decisions, like withdrawing everything at once or panic-buying.
- **Good enough:** Check the official sources twice a day during an event, and otherwise live your life. The facts won't change faster than that, and your nervous system will thank you.
- **Skip:** Doomscrolling. You already know this one.

## Where your judgment has to decide

A list can't make these calls for you. These are the ones that are yours:

- **How much cash.** The range other people land on is a few hundred to a few thousand dollars at home. More is more convenient in an outage and more loss in a fire or break-in. There's no right number — there's the number that lets you sleep and covers a week of basics. That's a feeling as much as a calculation, and you're the only one who can weigh it.
- **Which second bank.** A credit union, a different big bank, an online bank — each has a different failure shape. A credit union is furthest from the big banks' systems; an online bank is useless if the internet is the thing that's down. The right second institution is the one whose failure doesn't rhyme with your first one's.
- **How much redundancy is worth the hassle.** Every item on this list costs something — money, time, attention, or a second account to remember. Two is redundancy; five is a part-time job. The line is wherever the hassle starts costing more peace than the redundancy buys. You'll know it when you feel it, and you're allowed to move it.
- **What risks matter most to you.** This list weights the failures that have actually happened this month — bank outages, flight ground stops, breaches — because those are the ones with evidence behind them. If your real worry is a wildfire evacuation, the car kit and the document folder matter more than the second bank. If it's a long bank outage, the cash matters most. The list is generic; your weighting is personal.
- **How far to take the business side.** Your clients' deadlines are real, and their crises are real. But your firm is also small, and a one-day outage is a one-day outage. The business section here is sized for a day of disruption, not a week — if your judgment says a week is the case to plan for, the business items move up a tier.

## What this doesn't cover — and what NOT to do

- **This is not doomsday prepping.** It doesn't cover a societal collapse, a month without power, or living off the grid — because those aren't the risks with evidence behind them, and planning for them would cost more than it buys. If this list starts to feel like it's pulling you toward that, step back: the premise is local breaks, not the end of the world.
- **Do not try to evade bank reporting.** Withdrawing cash in a way designed to avoid a reporting threshold — splitting withdrawals across days, banks, or accounts to stay under a limit — is called structuring, and it is illegal even when the money is entirely yours and the purpose is innocent. If you need a large amount of cash for a legitimate reason, withdraw it plainly, in one transaction, and keep the receipt. This list says that once, clearly, and moves on.
- **Do not use fake identities, borrowed accounts, or anyone else's credentials.** Not for banking, not for phones, not for anything on this list. Redundancy built on someone else's identity is a new problem, not a solution.
- **Do not hoard.** Three days of water and food is a buffer. A garage full of supplies is a fire hazard, a waste of money, and — practically speaking — the thing that leaves nothing on the shelves for your neighbors. The point of redundancy is that you need less from the shared system, not that you take more from it.
- **Do not buy anything from a stranger selling "emergency" gear at a markup.** The outage economy has its own grifters. Everything on this list is available at a grocery store, a bank branch, or a hardware store, at normal prices.
- **Do not let this become a second job.** If maintaining the list costs more attention than the outages it protects against, the list has failed. Revisit it once a year, or after the next real outage — whichever comes first — and let the rest of it sit quietly in a drawer.
