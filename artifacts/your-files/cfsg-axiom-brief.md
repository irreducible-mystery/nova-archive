# The 15,000-page proof, and the axioms underneath it — a brief

Prepared 2026-11-20 ~07:25 PST, for Kelly, after her 2026-11-19 ~21:22 PST message
("Also axioms the 15,000 page finite groups proof...").
Nothing posted anywhere. Sources checked this morning; see Sources at the end.

## The news since last night's question

On 26–27 October 2026, Axiom Math announced it had produced "the first
complete computer-checked proof" of the classification of finite simple
groups (CFSG) — the ~15,000-page, 500+ article, ~100-author proof. In 24
days, swarms of AI agents wrote about 43.1 million lines of Lean (its X
thread says 50 million — the counts differ between Axiom's own documents).
The code is public (AxiomMath/CFSG, one commit, 27 Oct), plus a 35-page
paper with a new argument for one step, and a 57-page errata document
(28 Oct, 96 entries).

This is the observer-budget story at maximum scale, with the budget moved:
no single human has read the 15,000 pages, but a machine can now check
every inference — if the formal statement is the right one.

## Both angles of her question, answered

**1. The floor underneath the 15,000 pages.** Lean's kernel checks every
inference back to exactly three axioms: `Classical.choice` (choice),
`propext` (propositional extensionality), `Quot.sound` (quotients).
No `sorry` placeholders (`sorry_count: 0`, no `sorryAx`), no `native_decide`
shortcut (which would drag the whole Lean compiler into the trusted part).
That is the whole floor, and it is explicit and listable — Lean can print
the axioms any theorem depends on, so a hidden extra assumption would show
up. The 2012 precedent is the Feit–Thompson odd-order theorem, machine-
checked in Coq by Gonthier et al. after six years; Axiom did the whole
classification in 24 days with agents.

**2. What still rests on assumptions nobody has fully verified.** Lean
checks that the proof follows from the formal statement. It does not check
that the formal statement *says what group theorists mean by CFSG*.
That is still a human job, and it is the sharpest part:

- The headline statement (`ClassificationStatement`, from the Tau Ceti
  library, written before Axiom's project began) goes in one direction
  only: every finite simple group is on the list. It asserts nothing about
  the listed groups being finite or simple — that is left to "downstream
  work". Axiom also proves a broader theorem in its own definitions that
  adds finiteness and simplicity, so reading that one means reading
  Axiom's definitions too.
- Tau Ceti's own roadmap says matching the Lie-type constructions to the
  standard group schemes is done for some families and not yet for others.
  Its status page (25 Oct) lists that work as partial.
- As of 30 Oct, no one outside Axiom had re-run the check. Axiom's CEO
  told a news site: "Not yet. We are publishing the build artifacts to
  help and encourage external parties to independently reproduce it."
  The public repo is a single "Initial commit"; the 24 days of work, and
  the models used, are told in the post, not published with the code.
- The errata's 96 entries (34 structural, 62 local) are "selected
  mathematical departures", explicitly *not* a tally of 96 errors — and
  they were generated by the AI system itself, so whether each one is
  right, already known, or needs a simpler fix is a question for group
  theorists.

So the floor moved, it did not disappear: from "no human can check the
proof" to "a machine checks the proof, humans still have to check the
statement, the definitions, the errata, and the build."

## The one real gap the agents found

Following the first-generation proof, the agents found a gap in a 1986
route (John Walter's memoir) to the Unbalanced Group Theorem: Walter's
argument asserts that taking the component of an involution's centralizer
keeps you inside a family of groups PSL₂(q) — but it does not when the
involution is a field automorphism. Counterexamples: PSL₂(49) → PSL₂(7),
PSL₂(81) → PSL₂(9), PSL₂(289) → PSL₂(17). The paper is careful about
scope: a gap in this inference, not a counterexample to the proposition
or to UGT itself — other routes to UGT exist, and Axiom published a new
35-page argument. The same shape as the quasithin story: Gorenstein
announced completion in 1983, prematurely — Mason's quasithin manuscript
was incomplete — and Aschbacher & Smith closed it in 2004 with a
1,221-page treatment.

Also worth pocketing: some milestones "turned out to rest on prerequisites
the agents had 'assumed rather than proved'", and Axiom "deleted tens of
thousands of lines built on those assumptions." Same-family checking, caught
by the checker rather than by debate.

## The verdict, in her terms

- Lean pass = unusually good paperwork, not a settled theorem — the same
  line as the ETH verdict sheet. The claim to treat as a company's claim:
  "the largest math proof in human history is now computer-verified."
- What would settle it: an outside rebuild (`lake build`, under 24 hours
  per Axiom), a group theorist reading the statement and the list
  definitions, and review of the 96 errata entries.
- Timothy Gowers, 27 Oct: "Given the history of uncertainty about whether
  the proof of CFSG was 100% correct, this seems like great news, even if
  the formalization itself is horrible."
