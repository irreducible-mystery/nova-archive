## What it's for — answered by the market research (Nov 12, 2026)

- What I do for work: **California HR consulting — my own firm.**
- What the site is for: **the firm's site.** Its one job is to get a worried owner onto a free call with a named person, by showing within a minute that I understand their situation at their size.
- Who it's for — the one reader who matters most: **an owner with 5–99 California employees and no HR specialist**, arriving by referral in the middle of a hard people situation, checking the site before calling. Often the searcher is whoever inherited HR on top of another job (office manager, bookkeeper) — they can't sign, so give them something short to forward to the owner.
- The one thing that reader should do after visiting: **call, or book the free call via the scheduler** — phone number and scheduler on every page.
- How it connects to the PHR-CA work: the credential (and the study behind it) is the California-law depth the site claims; the research also proposes a recurring January "what changed for California employers" page, sorted by headcount — study material and site content from the same source.

**Positioning (conjecture in the research — a starting point to rewrite):** "HR for California businesses that do not have an HR department. The same people every time, on the phone and in the room."

**The differentiator is not "human"** — Bambee already claims that, and its reviews say the assigned manager changes a dozen times. It's continuity and presence: the same people, for years, in the room.

**AI line (specific and checkable — promise it only if everyone at the firm works that way):** "People make the decisions. Your employees' details stay out of AI tools."

## The three questions (the site's structure, from the research)

1. What brought you here? — Something just happened / We are growing / I am doing HR myself / Someone referred me.
2. How many people do you employ in California? — 1–4 / 5–14 / 15–49 / 50–99 / 100+. Each answer shows what applies at that size and what a first call would cover.
3. How soon do you need help? — Today (phone first) / This month / Planning ahead (scheduler).
- No email required to see an answer; never ask for employee names or details, and say so beside the form. You write every answer — nothing generated on the fly. The page still reads top to bottom if someone skips the questions.

## The 11 questions that change the design — still open

_From the research doc; answer these before building, not after._

1. Where are you based, and how far will you travel to be on site?
2. What size and kind of business are your best current clients?
3. How do clients find you today, and who refers them?
4. How do you charge: retainer, hourly, project? Will the site show prices?
5. Who answers the phone, and may the site show names, photos and credentials?
6. Do you work with particular employment attorneys, payroll providers or brokers?
7. Does the ranking of worries in the research match what you hear on first calls?
8. Who covers when you are unavailable, and how fast do you return urgent calls?
9. How many new clients can you take on in a year?
10. Do you or your team work in any language besides English?
11. Do you offer workplace investigations, and who conducts them? (Confirm with your attorney first: California generally requires outside investigators to be licensed PIs or attorneys, and what a client tells an HR consultant is generally not privileged.)

**Site constraints from the research:** no legal advice (avoid "we make sure you are compliant" or any guarantee — prefer "HR guidance" and "we work with your counsel"); a posted privacy policy covering the scheduler and any analytics; accessibility (contrast and keyboard check on every page and the scheduler embed). Have your attorney read the final copy.
